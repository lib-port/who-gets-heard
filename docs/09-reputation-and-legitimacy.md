# Chapter 9: Reputation and Legitimacy

## The sentence that arrived first

When Elena joins a regional planning team, her new manager tells her, “You should know that people find Marcus difficult.” Marcus leads forecasting for one of the smaller business units. The warning is not connected to a particular event. It has no date, example, or response from Marcus. It arrives as useful background—the kind of advice a manager might share to help a new colleague avoid friction.

Two weeks later, Marcus challenges an optimistic sales assumption. His model shows that the plan depends on growth the region has never achieved. Elena notices that the room hears more than his analysis. His questions become “resistance.” A director says he is “doing the usual thing.” When Marcus asks which evidence would change the forecast, no one answers directly.

Marcus may indeed communicate badly. He may repeat objections after a decision, overlook commercial judgement, or exhaust colleagues with unnecessary detail. Elena cannot learn any of that from the label *difficult*. The label has done something else: it has supplied a ready-made explanation for whatever Marcus says next.

**Reputation** is the shared expectation people carry about someone's likely conduct or value. **Legitimacy** is the degree to which a person, role, process, or claim is treated as appropriate and entitled to be heard. Both are unavoidable. No organisation can reassess every person from first principles in every meeting. Experience helps people decide whose estimates to trust, who may approve a risk, and who follows through.

The danger begins when reputation substitutes for current evidence or legitimacy is granted and withdrawn through invisible standards. A useful reputation compresses relevant experience. A harmful one compresses rumour, status, isolated mistakes, and inherited opinion into a verdict.

## Three layers of a reputational claim

When someone describes a colleague as “unreliable,” “not strategic,” “unsafe,” “political,” or “hard to work with,” separate three layers:

1. **Observation:** What happened, when, and in which setting?
2. **Interpretation:** What did the observer think the event meant?
3. **Prediction:** What do people now expect the person to do?

These layers are often collapsed. “Marcus is obstructive” might refer to questioning three assumptions, missing one deadline, using a blunt tone, or opposing a favoured proposal. Each possibility requires different evidence and a different response.

The same discipline applies to positive labels. “High potential,” “trusted pair of hands,” and “leadership material” can open opportunities before anyone names the behaviour behind them. Favourable reputations may be earned, but their benefits can compound: greater visibility leads to stronger assignments, which create more evidence of capability. Sociologist Robert Merton used the “Matthew effect” to describe cumulative advantage in science, where established researchers may receive disproportionate recognition.[^1] Workplaces are not identical to scientific institutions, but the compounding mechanism is recognisable.

A fair system does not pretend that reputation is irrelevant. It makes consequential claims specific, reviewable, and open to revision.

Five recurring patterns frustrate that aim: repeating an unverified label, escalating before using a safe and available clarification step, introducing a late credibility doubt, moving expertise standards after hearing input, and invoking authority that has not been confirmed.

## 1. An unverified label becomes fact through repetition

One manager says an employee is “not ready.” Another copies the phrase into succession notes. A third uses it to explain why the employee was not invited to a client meeting. Eventually, those repeated decisions are treated as evidence that the original assessment must have been sound.

Informal workplace talk can transmit useful warnings, social norms, and information that formal systems miss. Research on workplace gossip describes constructive as well as harmful functions; effects depend on content, relationships, motives, and context.[^2] The problem is not that colleagues exchange impressions. It is that an unverified impression can acquire authority without a source, example, time limit, or opportunity to respond.

Before carrying a negative description into a consequential process, ask:

- Which behaviour does the label describe?
- Did the speaker observe it directly?
- How recent and repeated was it?
- Which expectation applied at the time?
- Did the person receive feedback and a chance to respond?
- Which evidence would update the assessment?

Replace character shorthand with bounded language. “Marcus challenged the forecast in four meetings after the steering group approved it” can be checked. It also allows context: perhaps the assumptions changed after approval, or perhaps Marcus continued a settled dispute. “Marcus is negative” cannot be tested and may spread into situations where it has no relevance.

Some labels involve serious safety, integrity, harassment, or safeguarding concerns. Do not investigate these casually or reveal confidential reports in the name of fairness. Use the authorised process, preserve necessary confidentiality, and distinguish protective interim measures from final findings. A responsible manager may need to restrict access before every fact is resolved. That restriction should still have a defined purpose, review point, and authorised owner.

If an imprecise label affects you, an immediate accusation of bias or malice may harden positions before you understand the source. Ask for operational meaning: “Which specific behavior should I change?” “Can you give me a recent example?” “How will we know the concern is resolved?” A manager who cannot reveal confidential details may still be able to state the expectation and the process being followed.

## 2. Escalation precedes safe clarification

Escalation changes both the audience and the meaning of an event. A confusing email becomes a conduct concern when forwarded to a director. A disputed number becomes “a refusal to cooperate” in a formal note. Once senior people are involved, the recipient may feel pressure to defend the escalation rather than revisit the misunderstanding.

Early escalation can be appropriate. Direct conversation is not a universal requirement. Threats, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, fraud, serious safety risks, power imbalances, evidence-destruction concerns, or explicit reporting rules may require a protected or formal route. People should not be told to confront someone who may harm them or control their employment.

For ordinary ambiguity, however, a safe and available clarification step improves the evidence. It can test whether the issue is a misunderstanding, conflicting instruction, missing context, or genuine refusal:

> I read your message as saying the deadline no longer applies. Is that what you intended?

> The decision log assigns the analysis to my team, while your note assigns it to yours. Can we confirm the owner?

> I may be missing context. Which risk are you asking us to accept?

The purpose is not compulsory harmony. It is to improve the evidence before reputational stakes rise.

When escalation is necessary, make it factual and proportionate. State the issue, impact, safe steps already attempted, and the decision or help required. “We have conflicting ownership records and could not resolve them by Thursday; please confirm the accountable role” is more useful than “Marcus is being impossible again.”

Managers receiving an escalation should not reward dramatic framing. Ask what happened, what was attempted, which urgency exists, and which authority is needed. Do not automatically send the matter back for direct conversation—or accept the first account as complete.

## 3. A late credibility doubt becomes a quiet veto

Timing matters. A concern about someone's judgement may be legitimate at any stage, but a vague doubt raised just before a vote, promotion, contract award, or approval can shape the outcome without time for examination.

Imagine that Marcus is being considered to lead a forecasting redesign. The day before selection, a director says, “I'm not sure the business trusts him.” The claim is consequential but elastic. Which business leaders? Trust him to do what? Based on which events? The panel may exclude Marcus to avoid risk even though no one can answer those questions.

Social hierarchies influence how groups allocate attention and credibility. Status can serve as a shortcut for competence; hierarchy can also help coordinate work when it reflects relevant differences and remains open to correction.[^3] A late credibility doubt often gains force from the speaker's status rather than the evidence.

Apply a relevance test:

- Is the concern tied to a criterion announced for this decision?
- Is it specific enough to assess?
- Would the same evidence be accepted about another candidate or proposal?
- Can the affected person respond without exposing protected information?
- Should the decision pause, proceed with conditions, or disregard the claim?

Do not ignore every late concern. A newly discovered conflict, misconduct allegation, reference problem, or material risk may justify a pause and qualified review. Record why the process changed and which interim decision was made. The safeguard is not an inflexible deadline but traceable handling.

Nor should a vague concern operate as a secret veto. If the claim is too indefinite to test and no urgent protective duty applies, the panel can state: “We have heard a general concern but have no decision-relevant evidence. We will not treat it as an adverse finding.” That sentence protects the decision and the people involved.

## 4. Expertise standards move after input arrives

Expertise rarely comes from a single credential. It may arise from education, professional certification, long practice, local knowledge, lived experience, analytical skill, or responsibility for the consequences. Different questions require different combinations.

A moving standard appears when the requirement changes after someone contributes. A junior analyst's model is dismissed because she lacks field experience; when an experienced operator supports it, the model is dismissed because he lacks formal training. A customer representative is told that only technical experts can assess a design; after technical experts raise the same concern, leaders say the decision is commercial rather than technical.

The change may be innocent. New information can reveal that another kind of expertise is needed. The question is whether the shift is explained and applied consistently.

Define the question and relevant expertise before seeking views where possible:

- engineers assess whether the design meets the technical tolerance;
- service staff explain operating conditions and failure patterns;
- finance tests cost assumptions; and
- the accountable executive decides the trade-off within approved risk limits.

No contributor must be authoritative on every aspect for their evidence to matter. Someone can be wrong about the preferred solution and right about a factual constraint. Evaluate the claim at the level where it is made.

When the required expertise genuinely changes, state why: “The engineering test is complete. We are now deciding whether the residual risk is commercially acceptable, which belongs to the product owner under the policy.” Record the transition so that disagreement is not retroactively recast as illegitimate participation.

Watch for asymmetry. Are formal qualifications demanded only from outsiders while familiar insiders rely on experience? Is lived experience welcomed ceremonially but ignored in design? May senior generalists overrule specialists without stating the competing value or authority? Consistency does not require equal decision rights. It requires an intelligible link among the question, the evidence, and the authorised decision-maker.

## 5. Unconfirmed authority ends the discussion

Legitimacy can be borrowed. Someone says, “Legal requires this,” “The regulator will not allow it,” “The CEO has decided,” or “The customer expects it.” The named authority may be real, misunderstood, outdated, or absent. Because few people want to challenge law, executives, regulators, or customers, the statement can close debate.

Not every claim needs a signed letter. Work depends on delegated conversations and professional summaries. A sales lead may accurately relay a customer constraint, counsel may advise orally, and executives may authorise a direction before minutes exist. The level of verification should match the consequence.

For a material decision, confirm four things:

1. **Source:** Who or what is the authority?
2. **Scope:** What exactly was required, advised, or preferred?
3. **Currency:** When was it stated, and does it still apply?
4. **Decision right:** Does the source decide, advise, set a boundary, or merely influence?

Legal advice may identify risk without dictating the business decision. A published regulatory rule differs from one employee's prediction of regulatory reaction. A customer preference differs from a contractual obligation. A chief executive's strategic aim may not determine the design choice under discussion.

Ask neutrally: “Can we see the requirement or confirm it with the policy owner?” “Is that a legal prohibition, a risk recommendation, or our internal preference?” “Who received the customer's instruction, and what wording did they use?” The purpose is to locate the real constraint, not embarrass the messenger.

If confirmation must wait, record both assumption and consequence: “Proceeding on the unverified assumption that data must remain in-country; privacy counsel to confirm by Tuesday.” Visible uncertainty is less likely to harden into permanent folklore.

Falsely invoking authority may be misconduct in some settings. More often, people overstate because they want speed, misremember a conversation, or turn advice into a rule. Begin with verification unless the risk requires a formal response.

## Status changes the burden of proof

The same sentence lands differently depending on who speaks. A senior leader may offer an intuition that others treat as a conclusion. A less established employee may need a deck, model, and several endorsements to make the equivalent point. That difference is not always irrational: leaders may have wider context and carry different accountability. Status can nevertheless become an invisible substitute for evidence.

Resource-dependence research and studies of organisational power show that control of valued resources and formal position influence whose preferences carry weight.[^4] Research on incivility also describes how low-intensity disrespect can spiral through reciprocal responses, making a relationship progressively harder to repair.[^5] A dismissive remark from a powerful person can therefore do more than hurt; it signals whose participation may be safely discounted.

Managers can counter this effect without pretending hierarchy does not exist. Ask the highest-status speaker to state their evidence. Invite the person closest to the work to speak first. Separate recommendation from decision. Protect dissenters from being reduced to their delivery style while still addressing genuinely harmful behaviour.

The aim is not equal deference to every claim. It is a fair route by which claims gain or lose weight.

## Reasonable caution is not reputational sabotage

A useful framework must leave room for warning. Managers have duties to share relevant performance information, protect employees and customers, assess suitability, provide references within governing rules, and respond to risk. Colleagues also rely on candid advice.

The difference lies in discipline:

- relevant behaviour rather than totalising character;
- an authorised audience rather than broad circulation;
- necessary rather than entertaining detail;
- current evidence rather than permanent stigma;
- a stated purpose rather than social bonding at someone's expense; and
- a path to review, correction, or expiry where appropriate.

Confidentiality prevents perfect symmetry. The subject of a complaint may not receive every identity or document. That does not make every secret claim valid. Authorised reviewers can test evidence, disclose what fairness and law require, and avoid using unresolved allegations for unrelated purposes.

The framework also leaves room for judgement. A hiring manager does not need a courtroom finding to conclude that a candidate's examples do not meet the role. They should apply job-relevant criteria, comparable scrutiny, and an accurate record—not diagnose the candidate's character.

## A response ladder for a damaging label

- **Translate.** Ask which observable behaviour the label describes and why it matters to the current decision.
- **Locate.** Identify the source, date, audience, and whether the account is first-hand.
- **Compare.** Check how similar behaviour and evidence have been treated for people in comparable roles.
- **Clarify safely.** Seek the affected person's response when appropriate while respecting protected reporting and confidentiality.
- **Bound the use.** Decide whether the information is relevant, reliable enough, current, and authorised for this purpose.
- **Correct the record.** Replace or supplement inaccurate shorthand; note unresolved disagreement rather than manufacture certainty.
- **Escalate proportionately.** Use a formal route when the allegation, risk, repetition, or power imbalance requires one.

If you are affected personally, keep a factual record of decisions, dates, feedback, and requests for clarification. Do not collect private messages you are not entitled to access or launch a counter-rumour campaign. Seek help from a manager, employee representative, human-resources adviser, ombuds function, or qualified adviser when the stakes warrant it.

## Repairing a reputation is not deleting history

Once a reputational judgement affects work, telling people to “start fresh” is rarely enough. The old expectation still influences assignments, interpretations of ambiguous conduct, and who is asked for an opinion. Repair needs an active route from a broad conclusion to new, relevant evidence.

Begin by defining the claim. Suppose a manager calls an employee unreliable because three client deliverables were late. Establish which dates were agreed, what the employee controlled, what changed, and how comparable work was assessed. If the concern is supported, translate it into a bounded expectation: during the next two client cycles, confirm dependencies at launch, flag forecast delay within one working day, and meet agreed milestones unless the owner approves a change.

Then provide a fair opportunity to demonstrate the expectation. Asking someone to prove readiness while withholding the work through which readiness can be shown creates a closed loop. The assignment need not carry unrestricted risk. It can include staged responsibility, defined check-ins, a mentor, or review at key points. Support does not prove the original concern false; it is part of responsible performance management.

Decide in advance which evidence will update the judgement and when review will occur. “Reputation improves over time” leaves the person waiting on an invisible clock. A manager can say: “We will review these two projects in September using delivery, escalation, client feedback, and the agreed dependencies. If the evidence is sustained, the reliability concern will no longer limit assignment eligibility.” Accurate records may still be retained under governing rules. The aim is to stop an old summary from operating indefinitely without current relevance.

Repair may require correcting another person's conduct. A manager who circulated an unsupported characterisation should update the authorised recipients: “I previously described Marcus as resistant. That was too broad. We are addressing specific communication issues, but his forecast challenge was supported by changed assumptions and should not be treated as refusal to collaborate.” This is not a public apology tour. It is a proportionate correction along the path the error travelled.

The affected person has responsibilities too. They may need to acknowledge supported behaviour, use the agreed feedback, and avoid demanding that every colleague erase genuine experience. Trust is not restored by declaration. It grows through relevant conduct, a fair chance to display it, and observers willing to update.

Sometimes no shared account is possible. A manager and employee may disagree about past events after a careful process. Record the specific disagreement and focus forward: the current standard, support, evidence period, and review route. A precise unresolved record is better than forced agreement or a permanent character verdict.

Reputation repair also applies to teams. A function called “bureaucratic” may have inherited poorly designed controls. A department called “uncommercial” may be measured only on risk. Shared service measures, reciprocal shadowing, visible response standards, and successful joint work can replace caricature with experience. Slogans about partnership rarely can.

## For managers: make reputation revisable

Begin with consequential talent labels. Terms such as “high potential,” “not ready,” “trusted,” and “performance concern” need an operational meaning, evidence period, decision owner, and review point. People need not see confidential comparative deliberations to receive useful expectations and feedback.

In selection and promotion, distinguish threshold requirements from preferences. Record the evidence behind the decision, including any late material concern and how it was handled. Do not build a permanent shadow file from untested impressions.

In references and handovers, state role-relevant facts and follow applicable law and policy. “She sometimes struggled to manage competing deadlines during the fourth-quarter launch; we agreed weekly prioritization and delivery improved” conveys more than “not resilient.”

Watch for labels that travel farther than their evidence. Conflict with one manager should not automatically define a person's ability to work with every team. A mistake in one technical domain should not become “poor judgment” without limits. Positive reputation also needs review: are favoured people still benefiting from old success while current contribution goes untested?

Finally, make correction visible enough to work. If a review clears a material allegation or new evidence changes an assessment, update the systems and decision-makers that received the earlier account where lawful and appropriate. A private correction cannot repair a widely distributed label.

## Returning to Marcus

Elena asks her manager what “difficult” means. The manager identifies two concerns: Marcus interrupts commercial leads, and he continued circulating objections after a forecast was approved. Elena checks the project record. Marcus did send three follow-up analyses, but the underlying sales assumptions changed after approval. Two colleagues confirm that his meeting style can be blunt.

The team now has two separate matters. Marcus receives specific feedback about interruption and agrees to submit questions before planning meetings. The planning director establishes a rule for reopening forecasts when assumptions change. Marcus's technical challenge is assessed against the model rather than his reputation.

Elena has not proved that every earlier criticism was unfair. She has done something more practical: she has prevented a broad label from deciding a new question by itself.

## Practice: unpack one label

Choose a consequential label you have heard or used at work. Do not select a matter that belongs in a protected investigation. Write:

1. the exact label;
2. the observable behaviour it may describe;
3. the source and date of your information;
4. the work consequence the label is influencing;
5. one plausible alternative explanation;
6. the evidence that would confirm, narrow, or update it; and
7. a more precise sentence you could use instead.

The purpose is not to neutralise every judgement. It is to make judgements specific enough to examine and fair enough to revise.
