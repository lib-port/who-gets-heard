# Chapter 9: Reputation and Legitimacy

## The sentence that arrived first

When Elena joins a regional planning team, her new manager tells her, “You should know that people find Marcus difficult.” Marcus leads forecasting for a smaller business unit. The warning comes without a date, example, or response from Marcus. It is presented as useful background to help a new colleague avoid friction.

Two weeks later, Marcus challenges an optimistic sales assumption. His model shows that the plan requires growth the region has never achieved. Elena notices that colleagues interpret his analysis through the earlier label. His questions become “resistance.” A director says he is “doing the usual thing.” When Marcus asks what evidence would change the forecast, no one answers directly.

Marcus may communicate badly. He may repeat objections after a decision, overlook commercial judgement, or overwhelm colleagues with unnecessary detail. Elena cannot establish any of that from the label *difficult*. It has instead given her a ready-made explanation for whatever Marcus says next.

**Reputation** is the shared expectation people hold about someone’s likely conduct or value. **Legitimacy** is the extent to which a person, role, process, or claim is considered appropriate and entitled to be heard. Both are unavoidable. Organisations cannot assess every person afresh in every meeting. Experience helps people decide whose estimates to trust, who may approve a risk, and who follows through.

Problems arise when reputation replaces current evidence or legitimacy is granted and withdrawn under standards that people cannot see. A useful reputation summarises relevant experience. A harmful one turns rumour, status, isolated mistakes, and inherited opinions into a judgement about the person.

## Three layers of a reputational claim

When someone describes a colleague as “unreliable,” “not strategic,” “unsafe,” “political,” or “hard to work with,” separate three layers:

1. **Observation:** What happened, when, and in which setting?
2. **Interpretation:** What did the observer think it meant?
3. **Prediction:** What do people now expect the person to do?

These layers are often combined. “Marcus is obstructive” might mean that he questioned three assumptions, missed a deadline, spoke bluntly, or opposed a favoured proposal. Each requires different evidence and a different response.

Apply the same discipline to positive labels. “High potential,” “trusted pair of hands,” and “leadership material” can create opportunities before anyone explains the behaviour behind them. Favourable reputations may be earned, but their benefits can also accumulate: greater visibility leads to stronger assignments, which provide more evidence of capability. Robert Merton used the “Matthew effect” to describe cumulative advantage in science, where established researchers may receive disproportionate recognition.[^1] Workplaces differ from scientific institutions, but a similar compounding mechanism is recognisable.

A fair system recognises that reputation matters. It makes claims that affect decisions specific, reviewable, and open to revision.

Five recurring patterns undermine this aim: repeating unverified labels, escalating before a safe and available clarification step, raising late doubts about credibility, changing expertise standards after receiving input, and invoking unconfirmed authority.

## 1. An unverified label is treated as fact through repetition

One manager describes an employee as “not ready.” Another copies the phrase into succession notes. A third uses it to explain why the employee was excluded from a client meeting. Those decisions are eventually treated as evidence that the original assessment was sound.

Informal workplace talk can convey useful warnings, social norms, and information that formal systems miss. Research on workplace gossip identifies constructive and harmful functions, depending on the content, relationships, motives, and context.[^2] The problem arises when an unverified impression becomes an authoritative assessment without a source, example, time limit, or opportunity to respond.

Before using a negative description in a process with significant consequences, ask:

- Which behaviour does the label describe?
- Did the speaker observe it directly?
- How recent and repeated was it?
- Which expectation applied at the time?
- Did the person receive feedback and a chance to respond?
- Which evidence would update the assessment?

Describe specific conduct and its context. “Marcus challenged the forecast in four meetings after the steering group approved it” can be checked. It also leaves room for context: the assumptions may have changed after approval, or Marcus may have continued a settled dispute. “Marcus is negative” cannot be tested and may influence decisions in situations where the original concern is irrelevant.

Some labels concern serious safety, integrity, harassment, or safeguarding issues. Use the authorised process and preserve necessary confidentiality. Do not investigate casually or disclose confidential reports in the name of fairness. Distinguish interim protective measures from final findings. A manager may need to restrict access before every fact is resolved, but the restriction should have a defined purpose, review point, and authorised owner.

If an imprecise label affects you, accusing someone of bias or malice immediately may harden positions before you understand the source. Ask what the label means in practice: “Which specific behavior should I change?” “Can you give me a recent example?” “How will we know the concern is resolved?” Even when a manager cannot disclose confidential details, they may be able to explain the expectation and the process being followed.

## 2. Escalation precedes safe clarification

Escalation changes who hears about an event and how they understand it. A confusing email becomes a conduct concern when forwarded to a director. A disputed number becomes “a refusal to cooperate” in a formal note. Involving senior people can create pressure to defend the escalation instead of reconsidering the misunderstanding.

Early escalation can be appropriate. Direct conversation is not always required. Threats, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, fraud, serious safety risks, power imbalances, concerns about evidence destruction, or explicit reporting rules may require a protected or formal route. People should not be told to confront someone who may harm them or control their employment.

For ordinary ambiguity, a safe and available clarification step can establish whether the issue involves a misunderstanding, conflicting instructions, missing context, or genuine refusal:

> I read your message as saying the deadline no longer applies. Is that what you intended?

> The decision log assigns the analysis to my team, while your note assigns it to yours. Can we confirm the owner?

> I may be missing context. Which risk are you asking us to accept?

Clarification improves the evidence before the issue has greater reputational consequences. It does not require everyone to agree.

When escalation is necessary, state the issue, its impact, any safe steps already attempted, and the decision or help needed. “We have conflicting ownership records and could not resolve them by Thursday; please confirm the accountable role” gives the recipient more useful information than “Marcus is being impossible again.”

Managers should assess the facts behind an escalation. Dramatic wording should not give a claim extra weight. Ask what happened, what was attempted, why the issue is urgent, and what authority is needed. Avoid automatically returning the matter for direct conversation or accepting the first account as complete.

## 3. A late credibility doubt becomes a quiet veto

A concern about someone’s judgement may be legitimate at any stage. But a vague doubt raised just before a vote, promotion, contract award, or approval can influence the outcome before anyone has time to examine it.

Suppose Marcus is being considered to lead a forecasting redesign. The day before selection, a director says, “I'm not sure the business trusts him.” The claim matters, but its meaning is unclear. Which business leaders? Trust him to do what? Based on which events? The panel may exclude Marcus to avoid risk even though no one can answer.

Social hierarchies influence whose contributions receive attention and credibility. Status can be used as a shortcut for judging competence. Hierarchy can also help coordinate work when it reflects relevant differences and remains open to correction.[^3] A late credibility concern often carries weight because of the speaker’s status rather than the supporting evidence.

Test the concern’s relevance:

- Is it tied to a criterion announced for this decision?
- Is it specific enough to assess?
- Would the same evidence be accepted about another candidate or proposal?
- Can the affected person respond without exposing protected information?
- Should the decision pause, proceed with conditions, or disregard the claim?

Late concerns should not be dismissed automatically. A newly discovered conflict, misconduct allegation, reference problem, or material risk may justify a pause and review by someone qualified to assess it. Record why the process changed and what interim decision was made. The safeguard is a record of how the concern was handled, rather than an inflexible deadline for raising it.

A vague concern should not become an undisclosed reason for exclusion. If it is too indefinite to assess and no urgent protective duty applies, the panel can state: “We have heard a general concern but have no decision-relevant evidence. We will not treat it as an adverse finding.” This protects both the decision and the people involved.

## 4. Expertise standards change after input arrives

Expertise rarely comes from one credential. It may come from education, professional certification, long practice, local knowledge, lived experience, analytical skill, or responsibility for the consequences. Different questions require different combinations.

Expertise requirements can change after someone contributes. A junior analyst’s model is dismissed because she lacks field experience. When an experienced operator supports it, the model is dismissed because he lacks formal training. A customer representative is told that only technical experts can assess a design. When those experts raise the same concern, leaders say the decision is commercial rather than technical.

A change may have a legitimate explanation: new information can show that another kind of expertise is needed. The test is whether the change is explained and applied consistently.

Where possible, define the question and relevant expertise before seeking views:

- engineers assess whether the design meets the technical tolerance;
- service staff explain operating conditions and failure patterns;
- finance tests cost assumptions; and
- the accountable executive decides the trade-off within approved risk limits.

A contributor does not need expertise in every aspect for their evidence to matter. Someone may be wrong about the preferred solution and right about a factual constraint. Assess each claim within its scope.

When the expertise required genuinely changes, explain why: “The engineering test is complete. We are now deciding whether the residual risk is commercially acceptable, which belongs to the product owner under the policy.” Record the transition so earlier disagreement is not later treated as participation that should never have occurred.

Check whether standards vary with the contributor. Are formal qualifications required from outsiders while familiar insiders can rely on experience? Is lived experience welcomed in principle but ignored in design? Can senior generalists overrule specialists without stating the competing value or the authority for doing so? People need not have equal decision rights, but the link between the question, the evidence, and the authorised decision-maker should be clear.

## 5. Unconfirmed authority ends the discussion

People can give a claim authority by attributing it to someone else: “Legal requires this,” “The regulator will not allow it,” “The CEO has decided,” or “The customer expects it.” The authority may be real, misunderstood, outdated, or absent. Because people may hesitate to challenge law, executives, regulators, or customers, the claim can end discussion.

Written confirmation is not always necessary. Work depends on delegated conversations and professional summaries. A sales lead may accurately relay a customer constraint, counsel may advise orally, and executives may authorise a direction before minutes exist. Match the level of verification to the consequences of the decision.

For a material decision, confirm four things:

1. **Source:** Who or what is the authority?
2. **Scope:** What exactly was required, advised, or preferred?
3. **Currency:** When was it stated, and does it still apply?
4. **Decision right:** Does the source decide, advise, set a boundary, or merely influence?

Legal advice may identify risk without determining the business decision. A published regulatory rule differs from an employee’s prediction of a regulator’s response. A customer preference differs from a contractual obligation. A chief executive’s strategic aim may not determine the design choice being discussed.

Ask neutrally: “Can we see the requirement or confirm it with the policy owner?” “Is that a legal prohibition, a risk recommendation, or our internal preference?” “Who received the customer's instruction, and what wording did they use?” The aim is to establish the actual constraint without embarrassing the messenger.

If confirmation must wait, record the assumption and its consequence: “Proceeding on the unverified assumption that data must remain in-country; privacy counsel to confirm by Tuesday.” Making the uncertainty explicit reduces the risk that an assumption becomes a permanent, unquestioned rule.

Falsely invoking authority may be misconduct in some settings. More often, people overstate authority to speed work up, misremember a conversation, or turn advice into a rule. Begin by verifying the claim unless the risk requires a formal response.

## Status changes the burden of proof

The same claim can receive a different response depending on who makes it. A senior leader’s intuition may be treated as a conclusion, while a less established employee may need a presentation, model, and several endorsements to make the same point. There may be valid reasons for different treatment: leaders may have wider context and different accountability. Status can nevertheless become an unstated substitute for evidence.

Research on resource dependence and organisational power shows that control of valued resources and formal position influence whose preferences carry weight.[^4] Research on incivility describes how low-intensity disrespect can provoke reciprocal responses, making a relationship progressively harder to repair.[^5] A dismissive remark from a powerful person can therefore do more than hurt someone. It can signal that others may discount that person’s participation without consequence.

Managers can address this effect while recognising that hierarchy exists. Ask the highest-status speaker to explain their evidence. Invite the person closest to the work to speak first. Separate the recommendation from the decision. Ensure that a dissenter’s delivery style does not become the only basis for judging their contribution, while still addressing genuinely harmful behaviour.

Claims need not receive equal weight. They need a fair process for assessing how much weight they deserve.

## Reasonable caution is not reputational sabotage

People need to be able to share relevant warnings. Managers have duties to communicate performance information, protect employees and customers, assess suitability, provide references within governing rules, and respond to risk. Colleagues also need candid advice.

Apply clear limits:

- describe relevant behaviour rather than the person’s entire character;
- share information with an authorised audience;
- include necessary detail, without adding details for entertainment;
- use current evidence and avoid permanent stigma;
- state the purpose instead of using the discussion to bond at someone’s expense; and
- provide a route to review, correction, or expiry where appropriate.

Confidentiality means people may have different access to information. The subject of a complaint may not receive every identity or document, but confidentiality alone does not validate a claim. Authorised reviewers can test the evidence, disclose what fairness and law require, and avoid using unresolved allegations for unrelated purposes.

Professional judgement still has a place. A hiring manager does not need a courtroom finding to conclude that a candidate’s examples fall short of the role’s requirements. They should apply job-relevant criteria, comparable scrutiny, and an accurate record. The task is to assess suitability, not diagnose the candidate’s character.

## A response ladder for a damaging label

- **Translate.** Ask which observable behaviour the label describes and why it matters to the current decision.
- **Locate.** Identify the source, date, audience, and whether the account is first-hand.
- **Compare.** Check how similar behaviour and evidence have been treated for people in comparable roles.
- **Clarify safely.** Seek the affected person’s response where appropriate, while respecting protected reporting and confidentiality.
- **Bound the use.** Decide whether the information is relevant, reliable enough, current, and authorised for this purpose.
- **Correct the record.** Replace or supplement inaccurate shorthand. Record unresolved disagreement rather than presenting uncertainty as fact.
- **Escalate proportionately.** Use a formal route when the allegation, risk, repetition, or power imbalance requires one.

If you are personally affected, keep a factual record of decisions, dates, feedback, and clarification requests. Do not collect private messages you are not entitled to access or respond by spreading rumours. When the stakes warrant it, seek help from a manager, employee representative, human-resources adviser, ombuds function, or qualified adviser.

## Repair reputations through current evidence

Once a reputational judgement affects work, asking people to “start fresh” is rarely enough. The old expectation still shapes assignments, interpretations of ambiguous conduct, and decisions about whose opinion to seek. Repair requires a way to replace broad conclusions with relevant new evidence.

First, define the concern. Suppose a manager calls an employee unreliable because three client deliverables were late. Establish the agreed dates, what the employee controlled, what changed, and how comparable work was assessed. If the concern is supported, set a specific expectation. During the next two client cycles, the employee should confirm dependencies at launch, flag a forecast delay within one working day, and meet agreed milestones unless the owner approves a change.

Give the person a fair opportunity to demonstrate that they can meet the expectation. Withholding the work needed to demonstrate readiness prevents them from showing what they can do. The assignment can limit risk through staged responsibility, defined check-ins, a mentor, or review at key points. Providing support does not establish that the original concern was false; it is part of responsible performance management.

Agree in advance what evidence will change the assessment and when it will be reviewed. “Reputation improves over time” gives the person no clear standard or timetable. A manager can say: “We will review these two projects in September using delivery, escalation, client feedback, and the agreed dependencies. If the evidence is sustained, the reliability concern will no longer limit assignment eligibility.” Accurate records may still be retained under governing rules. The purpose is to prevent an old summary from restricting opportunities indefinitely after it loses current relevance.

Repair may also require correcting someone else’s conduct. A manager who circulated an unsupported characterisation should update the authorised recipients: “I previously described Marcus as resistant. That was too broad. We are addressing specific communication issues, but his forecast challenge was supported by changed assumptions and should not be treated as refusal to collaborate.” The correction should be proportionate and reach the people who received the error.

The affected person also has responsibilities. They may need to acknowledge supported concerns about their behaviour, act on agreed feedback, and accept that colleagues retain genuine experience. Trust grows through relevant conduct, a fair opportunity to demonstrate it, and observers willing to revise their assessments. It cannot be restored by declaration alone.

A careful process may still leave a manager and employee disagreeing about past events. Record the specific disagreement, then clarify the current standard, support, evidence period, and review route. A precise record of unresolved issues is more useful than forced agreement or a permanent judgement of character.

Teams may also need to repair their reputations. A function labelled “bureaucratic” may have inherited poorly designed controls. A department called “uncommercial” may be measured only on risk. Shared service measures, opportunities to observe each other’s work, visible response standards, and successful joint work can give colleagues evidence to replace a caricature. Slogans about partnership rarely achieve this.

## For managers: make reputation revisable

Start with labels that influence talent decisions. Terms such as “high potential,” “not ready,” “trusted,” and “performance concern” need an explanation of what they mean in practice, an evidence period, a decision owner, and a review point. People can receive useful expectations and feedback without seeing confidential comparisons with others.

In selection and promotion, distinguish minimum requirements from preferences. Record the evidence behind the decision, including any late material concern and how it was handled. Do not create a permanent, undisclosed record of untested impressions.

In references and handovers, give role-relevant facts and follow applicable law and policy. “She sometimes struggled to manage competing deadlines during the fourth-quarter launch; we agreed weekly prioritization and delivery improved” conveys more useful information than “not resilient.”

Keep the scope of a label within the evidence. Conflict with one manager should not automatically define someone’s ability to work with every team. A mistake in one technical area should not become a general label of “poor judgment.” Positive reputations also need review: are favoured people still benefiting from old success while their current contribution goes untested?

Make corrections visible enough to take effect. If a review clears a material allegation or new evidence changes an assessment, update the systems and decision-makers that received the earlier account where lawful and appropriate. A private correction cannot repair a widely distributed label.

## Returning to Marcus

Elena asks her manager what “difficult” means. The manager identifies two concerns: Marcus interrupts commercial leads, and he continued circulating objections after a forecast was approved. Elena checks the project record. Marcus sent three follow-up analyses, but the underlying sales assumptions changed after approval. Two colleagues confirm that his meeting style can be blunt.

The team now has two separate matters to address. Marcus receives specific feedback about interruption and agrees to submit questions before planning meetings. The planning director establishes a rule for reopening forecasts when assumptions change. Marcus’s technical challenge is assessed against the model rather than his reputation.

Elena has not established that every earlier criticism was unfair. She has prevented a broad label from determining the answer to a new question without examining the evidence.

## Practice: unpack one label

Choose a label you have heard or used at work that affects someone’s work. Do not choose a matter that belongs in a protected investigation. Write:

1. the exact label;
2. the observable behaviour it may describe;
3. the source and date of your information;
4. the work consequence the label is influencing;
5. one plausible alternative explanation;
6. the evidence that would confirm, narrow, or update it; and
7. a more precise sentence you could use instead.

The exercise allows people to make judgements while making them specific enough to examine and fair enough to revise.
