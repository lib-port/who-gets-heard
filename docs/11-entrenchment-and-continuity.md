# Chapter 11: Entrenchment and Continuity

## The person no one could work without

For nine years, Daniel has run the monthly settlement process at a logistics company. He knows which data feed fails after public holidays, which customer contracts permit manual adjustment, and which finance manager must approve an exception. When a reconciliation breaks, people call Daniel. He is patient, fast, and usually right.

His importance has grown gradually. Daniel now approves changes to the process he operates, trains the people who assist him, maintains the exception guide, and advises the committee that chooses his possible successor. Several departments have asked him to document the process. He always agrees, then delays because the next close is urgent. Proposed deputies spend months on routine tasks without learning the difficult cases, then rotate out.

When Daniel takes unexpected leave, settlement nearly stops. The company treats this as proof that it needs him even more. It gives him another retention payment and postpones a planned system change until he returns. Daniel has threatened no one. He may be protecting a fragile process with too little staffing. Yet the organisation has built a circle: his control creates dependence, and that dependence becomes the reason to preserve his control.

**Entrenchment** is influence that has become difficult to examine, transfer, or replace because authority, knowledge, relationships, and process are concentrated in one place. It can form around a founder, executive, administrator, specialist, vendor, committee, team, or software system. It is not inherently improper. Deep expertise and stable ownership often keep organisations functioning. Classic organisational research links power to managing important uncertainty and occupying central positions in dependence structures, helping explain why operational importance can become influence without a formal promotion.[^3][^4]

The governing test is continuity: can the organisation preserve legitimate service, evidence, and decision quality when a person changes role, becomes unavailable, disagrees, or leaves? If not, admiration for an indispensable contributor should coexist with concern about the design.

## Concentration is not the same as wrongdoing

A specialist may hold several responsibilities because the team is small. A crisis may require temporary command. A regulated role may need independence or exclusive authority. A confidential investigation may require a tightly limited group. A founder may reasonably make broad decisions early in an organisation's life.

Concentration becomes risky when it is difficult to see, lacks boundaries or review, and reproduces the conditions used to justify it. Ask:

- What is concentrated: formal authority, practical access, knowledge, relationships, or all four?
- Why is that concentration necessary?
- Which decisions require separation or independent review?
- Who can act during absence or conflict?
- How will the arrangement end or be reassessed?
- Which evidence would show that distribution is now possible?

The purpose is not to eliminate dependency. Organisations are networks of dependency. It is to prevent a valuable dependency from becoming the single point through which every correction, succession, and change must pass.

Five patterns deserve particular attention: accumulating decision rights, allowing one owner to control a complete process, narrowing succession through unclear criteria, concentrating history and operating knowledge, and repeatedly delaying documentation, delegation, or cross-training.

## 1. Decision rights accumulate quietly

Decision rights can accumulate without a formal reorganisation. A trusted specialist begins by recommending options. Because the advice is rarely challenged, colleagues start treating recommendation as approval. The specialist then controls the data used for the recommendation, chooses who attends the review, and confirms whether the resulting action is complete.

Each step may be convenient. Together, they collapse roles that provide useful challenge. One person defines the problem, selects the evidence, proposes the solution, authorises it, and reports success.

Map verbs rather than titles. For a consequential process, who may:

- request work;
- set priority;
- define eligibility or scope;
- supply or restrict information;
- recommend;
- approve, reject, or stop;
- implement;
- verify;
- change the rule;
- grant an exception;
- hear a challenge; and
- report the outcome?

A job description may say “process owner” while these verbs sit across several teams—or quietly with one person.

Separation matters most when error, self-interest, or abuse would be difficult to detect and costly to correct. Payment creation and approval are a familiar example. Others include hiring and final salary approval, system administration and access review, or sales credit and compensation calculation. Small organisations may not be able to separate every task. They can use compensating checks: independent sampling, dual approval above a threshold, immutable logs, rotating review, or external assurance.

Avoid decorative separation. If a verifier sees only the evidence selected by the process owner, or an approver lacks time to question a recommendation, the boxes are separate but the judgement is not. Reviewers need access, time, expertise, and authority.

Accumulated rights should have an origin. Was the authority delegated by policy, inherited after a departure, created during an emergency, or simply assumed? A sensible arrangement may still require formal recognition, boundaries, and a review date.

## 2. End-to-end ownership becomes unchecked control

Single ownership can create clarity. Product managers, case owners, and service leads exist because fragmented responsibility is frustrating. The distinction is between end-to-end accountability and unchecked end-to-end control.

An accountable owner should ensure that the process works. They need not personally control every gate, record, exception, and review. Daniel can remain accountable for settlement quality while another role approves high-value adjustments and a second person maintains access.

Look for a closed loop:

1. one owner defines the intake;
2. the owner decides which requests qualify;
3. the owner controls the queue and status information;
4. the owner approves exceptions;
5. complaints return to the owner; and
6. performance reports rely on measures chosen by the owner.

In such a loop, weak service may remain invisible because every fact passes through the same interpretation. This does not prove bad faith. Overloaded owners often simplify to survive; their local adaptations become rules because no other perspective exists.

Open the loop where a second view improves the evidence. Publish eligibility and service standards. Let requesters see status. Require reasons for material refusals. Route appeals elsewhere. Define measures jointly with users and an accountable sponsor. Sample closed cases. Provide an emergency alternative when the owner is unavailable.

Do not multiply checkpoints mechanically. Each handoff adds delay and can diffuse accountability. Use risk to decide where independence matters. A low-value routine request may need automated rules and audit sampling; a rare, irreversible exception may warrant two-person review.

The owner should participate in redesign. Treating them as the problem may destroy the tacit knowledge the organisation needs. Ask where they face overload, ambiguity, recurring exceptions, or weak upstream data. The person at the centre may want relief but fear a rushed handoff that leaves them responsible for someone else's failure.

## 3. Succession criteria narrow after candidates appear

Succession is not only the replacement of chief executives. Every role with scarce knowledge, decision authority, or critical relationships raises a continuity question: who could perform it tomorrow, and how could someone become ready?

An unclear process allows incumbents and sponsors to define readiness after seeing candidates. A potential successor is told that she needs international experience; after gaining it, she is told that she lacks senior-leadership exposure. Another is deemed ready because leaders “can see him in the role,” though no one explains the evidence.

Judgement is unavoidable. Future roles change, and leadership capability cannot be reduced to a checklist. The answer is not to pretend the decision is mathematical. It is to make the main requirements visible enough to support development and comparison.

Separate:

- **Eligibility:** Minimum requirements for consideration.
- **Readiness:** Evidence that a person could perform now or within a stated period.
- **Potential:** A forecast of capacity for larger or different work.
- **Preference:** A choice among qualified options based on current needs.
- **Risk:** Gaps and conditions that require support.

These ideas are often blurred. Someone may have strong long-term potential and not be ready today. A ready person may not be selected because another candidate better suits a particular transition. Preference should not be rewritten as proof that the unselected person was unqualified.

Use several sources: demonstrated work, structured examples, stakeholder experience, appropriate simulations, performance over time, and explicit development goals. Train reviewers to distinguish confidence and familiarity from evidence. Record material changes to criteria and apply them to every plausible candidate.

Incumbents hold essential insight into a role, but they should not have a private veto over succession. Their preferences may favour a familiar style, loyal deputy, or someone who will preserve current choices. For consequential appointments, include the role's manager, relevant partners, and an independent talent or governance perspective. Address conflicts openly.

Succession also depends on opportunity. A list of names is not a pipeline if only favoured candidates receive the acting assignments, executive exposure, and difficult decisions that create credibility. Chapter 1 described access; here the consequence is continuity. An organisation that withholds development may later cite the resulting lack of experience as a natural shortage. Research on workplace mentoring supports treating developmental relationships as a real part of the pipeline rather than an incidental benefit.[^5]

## 4. History and operating knowledge remain concentrated

Documents capture only part of what people know. Teams also rely on tacit knowledge: cues, relationships, exceptions, judgements, and physical or social routines learned through experience. A procedure may say “investigate differences above the threshold.” Daniel knows which difference signals a duplicated shipment, which results from currency timing, and whom to call before a customer's warehouse closes.

Research on transactive memory describes how groups develop shared knowledge about who knows what; performance may depend not only on individual memory but on a system for locating expertise.[^1] Organisational knowledge can also move through people, tools, routines, and networks rather than documents alone.[^2] Continuity therefore requires more than asking an expert to write everything down.

Knowledge concentration takes several forms:

- **Technical:** One person knows the code, calculation, equipment, or configuration.
- **Procedural:** One person knows the real sequence behind the formal process.
- **Relational:** One person holds customer, vendor, regulator, or internal trust.
- **Historical:** One person remembers why an exception or control exists.
- **Interpretive:** One person can distinguish a normal anomaly from an important one.
- **Access-based:** One person has the account, key, permission, or physical access needed to act.

Inventory these forms separately. A shared folder solves nothing if only one person can interpret its contents. A second password holder does not transfer judgement. Shadowing may reveal routines but miss rare exceptions. Build several paths: clear procedures, paired work, scenario rehearsal, decision records, contact handovers, supervised practice, access review, and rotation through real cases.

Prioritise by service impact, replaceability, learning time, and likelihood of loss. Do not demand exhaustive documentation from everyone; that creates a cemetery of outdated pages and signals distrust. Protect the activities whose loss would create serious harm, long delay, legal breach, or irreversible damage.

Make maintenance part of the work. Each critical guide needs an owner, review trigger, and evidence of use. A procedure last updated three years ago but used successfully by a deputy last month may be more trustworthy than a polished document no one has tested.

Relational continuity also needs care. Introducing a deputy to a customer or regulator does not transfer trust, but it creates a second route. Explain roles honestly. Do not use contact sharing to bypass confidentiality, professional duties, or commitments. Preserve institutional history without collecting unnecessary personal information.

## 5. Transfer is always postponed

Delay often sounds reasonable because it is. “After the quarter closes.” “Once the new system stabilizes.” “When we fill the vacancy.” Critical experts are usually busy precisely because others cannot perform their work. Creating redundancy adds effort before it removes any.

This creates a continuity trap:

1. dependence produces urgent demand on the expert;
2. urgent demand leaves no time for transfer;
3. failed transfer preserves dependence; and
4. preserved dependence produces more urgent demand.

Break the trap by funding transfer as delivery rather than treating it as an extra. Remove some operating work, narrow the first target, pair the expert with a named learner, and schedule a real test. The manager—not the expert alone—owns the trade-off.

Use small transfer units. Instead of “document settlement,” choose “enable a deputy to identify and route the five highest-impact reconciliation exceptions during Daniel's two-week absence.” Capture inputs, decision cues, actions, approval boundaries, contacts, evidence, and recovery steps. Then let the deputy perform the work while Daniel observes.

Delegation transfers a task or decision within stated boundaries. It is not dumping work while retaining every choice. Define what the delegate may decide, what requires consultation, what must be escalated, which resources are available, and how mistakes will be handled. If every non-routine case returns to the expert, the learner is an assistant rather than a successor.

Cross-training also needs depth. Watching a demonstration creates familiarity; capability requires retrieval, judgement, and action under realistic conditions. Use scenario drills, planned absence, peer review, and evidence from live work. Do not let practice become unpaid invisible labour or an indefinite acting role without recognition.

Some incumbents resist transfer because scarcity protects their status or employment. Others resist because earlier delegation created mistakes they had to repair, because indispensability is the only value leaders have recognised, or because leaders demand a handover while leaving them accountable for the result. Diagnose before moralising. Recognise teaching, adjust workload, protect role dignity, and make shared capability part of performance.

Persistent refusal after support, clarity, and reasonable time is different. When organisational knowledge transfer is a legitimate role requirement, a manager may need a formal performance or conduct response under policy. Preserve employee rights, confidentiality, intellectual-property terms, and professional obligations; not all knowledge may be copied or broadly shared.

## The indispensable-person bargain

Organisations often make an implicit bargain with key people: absorb impossible complexity, and we will excuse the controls that would make you less central. The person receives autonomy, status, or security. The organisation receives heroic recovery. Both postpone structural repair.

The bargain burdens the expert with constant interruption and limits their growth because no one can release them from the old work. It leaves colleagues underdeveloped, makes leaders reluctant to challenge the expert, and turns leave, illness, promotion, retirement, and ordinary disagreement into operational threats.

Appreciation and deconcentration can occur together: “Your knowledge has protected this service. We need to make that achievement durable and give you room for larger work.” Reward capable successors, tested processes, and shared relationships. Do not wait for departure to value transfer.

Retention remains legitimate. Some skills are scarce and costly to replace. Compensation, flexibility, career opportunity, and recognition may reduce avoidable loss. A retention plan is strongest when paired with continuity rather than used in place of it.

## Vendors, founders, committees, and systems can be entrenched

A vendor may control a proprietary format, administrative account, or implementation knowledge. A founder may hold customer trust and informal authority beyond the organisation chart. A committee may renew its own mandate for years. A legacy system may encode rules no one understands, allowing technical limitation to function like policy.

Use the same questions: what is controlled, how was the authority created, which evidence and alternatives exist, who can review it, and how can the organisation continue if the centre becomes unavailable?

For vendors, examine data portability, documentation, access, subcontractors, termination support, intellectual property, and recovery arrangements such as escrow where appropriate. Contract language is insufficient if no one has tested export or transition.

For founders and long-tenured leaders, formal governance may coexist with personal relationships that still determine outcomes. Clarify which decisions belong to the current role, move commitments into organisational records, broaden external relationships, and create a succession process that does not require the incumbent's private approval.

For systems, distinguish technical constraints from choices hidden behind “the system won't allow it.” Who configured the rule? May an authorised person change it? Which audit and exception path exists? A machine cannot be accountable for policy embedded in its settings.

## Transitions reveal the real operating system

Mergers, reorganisations, outsourcing, rapid growth, long leave, and planned exits expose dependencies that stable routines conceal. Leaders often begin with boxes and names. Continuity begins with services, decisions, knowledge, and relationships.

Before changing roles, identify what must remain true on day one. Which payments, care, access, customer responses, safety checks, approvals, and reporting duties cannot pause? Who performs them now in practice, not merely on the chart? Which workarounds and relationships make the formal process succeed?

Use structured handover conversations alongside documents. Ask the current owner to walk through a normal case, a difficult case, the last failure, the next likely decision, and the people whose context is essential. The incoming owner should restate the process and run a scenario. Differences reveal hidden assumptions better than a passive presentation.

Capture *why* along with *how*. A rule that appears irrational may protect against an earlier failure, fulfil a contract, or compensate for a system limit. It may also be obsolete. Record its origin, current owner, and review condition so a successor can question it without erasing history.

Relationship handover requires consent and dignity. Customers and partners are not transferable assets. Introduce the new contact, explain the continuing mandate, and create overlap where possible. Follow applicable confidentiality and consent requirements for sensitive professional, health, legal, or representative relationships.

Departing employees are vulnerable to unfair narratives. A rushed handover may be treated as evidence that they withheld knowledge even when leaders allowed no time. Define the required artefacts, access, meetings, and support early. Give the successor responsibility for confirming what they can use. Record gaps without guessing at motive.

Avoid coercive extraction. Employees should transfer organisational records and role knowledge as required, but continuity work must respect contracts, law, personal data, health, workload, and intellectual-property boundaries. Personal contacts, private devices, memories of confidential matters, and professional knowledge are not an unlimited corporate repository. Seek qualified advice in a contested exit.

After a transition, hold a thirty-day continuity review. Which decisions stalled? What could only the predecessor answer? Which documents were wrong? Which access arrived late? Which relationships need broader ownership? Correct the system before gaps harden around the new incumbent.

Do not make the replacement indispensable in the same way. Transition pressure encourages recentralisation. Establish the backup, shared record, independent check, and learning time while the role is being rebuilt. Continuity is easiest to design before emergency routines become tradition.

Handled well, a transition is more than a risk event. It can distinguish valuable expertise from accumulated workaround, preserve the former, remove the latter, and give the next owner a role that can be learned rather than inherited as a mystery.

## A response ladder for concentrated power

- **Map.** Identify decision verbs, critical knowledge, access, relationships, and review paths. Focus on a process rather than a personality.
- **Test absence.** Ask what fails after one day, one week, one month, and permanent departure. Use a tabletop exercise before risking live service.
- **Open one loop.** Add visibility, independent review, backup access, or a second route at the highest-risk point.
- **Fund transfer.** Give the incumbent and learner time, tools, recognition, and a concrete capability target.
- **Exercise.** Let the deputy perform real or simulated work; record gaps in both the guide and the learner's authority.
- **Reassign rights.** Separate incompatible duties and confirm delegated authority. Add proportionate compensating controls when staffing is limited.
- **Govern succession.** Publish relevant criteria, broaden development opportunities, manage conflicts, and record the basis of consequential choices.
- **Review.** Set a date and evidence for reducing the remaining concentration. Continuity work without review returns easily to emergency mode.

Escalate sooner when concentration enables financial abuse, safety risk, privacy breach, unauthorised access, evidence suppression, or legal or regulatory failure. Use qualified specialists and preserve records appropriately. Do not test a live control or access another person's account without authorisation.

## For managers: a ninety-day continuity experiment

Choose one critical activity. During the first thirty days, name the accountable owner, backup, essential inputs, decision boundaries, and failure impact. Observe a full cycle. Record the highest-impact exceptions and missing access. Protect time for both expert and learner.

During the next thirty days, the backup performs bounded work while the expert reviews. Update the guide through actual use. Introduce the backup to necessary partners. Move shared credentials into an authorised access system; never copy personal credentials informally. Confirm who may approve an emergency exception.

During the final thirty days, run a planned absence or tabletop exercise. The incumbent is unavailable for routine questions during a defined window but reachable through an emergency threshold. Measure completion, errors, delay, escalations, and the judgement the guide failed to capture.

At review, do not ask only, “Can the backup do it?” Ask whether the role design, tools, upstream data, workload, and decision rights make success possible. Choose the next unit of transfer. Reward both operational performance and reduced fragility.

For a senior role, use a longer horizon but the same logic. Succession candidates need development assignments, relationships, and decision practice before a vacancy. Emergency succession and long-term selection are different processes; document both.

## Returning to Daniel

Daniel's director stops treating the missing manual as his personal overdue task. She removes two reporting responsibilities for one quarter and names Mei, an experienced analyst, as deputy. Together, Daniel and Mei map the seven settlement exceptions with the greatest customer and financial impact.

Finance assumes approval of high-value adjustments. Technology moves shared configuration into a managed repository with backup administration. Daniel remains accountable for process quality, but a risk manager samples closed exceptions and the operations director hears appeals. Mei performs the next close while Daniel observes and intervenes only at agreed thresholds.

The exercise reveals that three steps in the old procedure are obsolete and that Daniel has been compensating for a broken data feed every month. Repairing the feed removes more dependency than another hundred pages of documentation would have done.

Daniel receives recognition for stabilising the system and building continuity. He is invited to lead the replacement design—a role he could not accept while every settlement depended on him. The organisation has not made his expertise less valuable. It has stopped requiring his constant presence as proof of that value.

## Practice: draw the continuity map

Choose a legitimate process you know well and are authorised to examine. For each important activity, record:

1. the role that decides;
2. the role that performs;
3. the knowledge or relationship on which the activity depends;
4. the access required;
5. the backup and the last time they performed the work;
6. the independent check, when one is warranted;
7. what would fail after a week of absence;
8. the smallest useful transfer exercise;
9. the time and support required; and
10. the date on which the concentration will be reviewed.

Do not score the people involved or circulate sensitive access details. The output is a service-continuity plan, not a suspicion map.

Continuity preserves both the work and its history. The next chapter examines how organisations turn that history into an account of what happened and what the outcome means.
