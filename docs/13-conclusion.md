# Conclusion: Building Influence Systems People Can Trust

## The organisation inside the meeting

Noor has just taken charge of a cross-functional service committee. It has a charter, a monthly calendar, and 12 senior members. On paper, its governance looks impressive. In practice, the important decisions happen elsewhere.

Two executives confer before each meeting and arrive with a preferred answer. Authors circulate papers late while waiting for their comments. A 40-minute presentation leaves 15 minutes for “discussion.” Frontline leaders attend only by invitation and for selected items. The programme manager’s minutes record agreement but omit members’ conditions. Actions go to whoever is present, whether or not they control the budget. Three months later, the committee judges progress through a dashboard chosen by the programme it is meant to oversee.

Noor could call this arrangement *political*. She would be right: relationships, authority, timing, information, and interpretation are shaping collective choices. But the label would not tell her what to repair.

She starts with the work. Every paper must name the decision, decision owner, options, criteria, affected roles, and sources for key claims. Pre-meetings may continue, but any material change they produce must appear in the circulated paper or be disclosed at the meeting. Each item begins with questions before the recommendation is debated. Minutes distinguish consultation from decision, and actions go only to roles that accept them. The dashboard gains a service measure chosen with frontline teams that also take into consideration unintended effects.

Influence remains. Executives still bring authority and wider context. Experts still frame technical issues. Skilled advocates still build support before the meeting. The system now makes it easier to see how those forces connect to evidence, responsibility, and correction.

Trust comes from on making these connections dependable in ordinary work.

## Politics is part of organising

Conflicting goals, scarce resources, distributed information, and uncertainty leave room for discretion, persuasion, coalitions, and trust. These conditions span multinational companies, public agencies, and family firms. Research accordingly treats organisational politics as perceptions, behaviours, actors, and outcomes rather than a few dramatic tactics.[^3]

Use the word *politics* to locate choices that need examination. It explains little when attached to every unpopular decision, and denying it can disguise contested choices between vested interests as equally necessary options judged against shifting criteria.

The chapters trace these choices from access and information through support, decisions, and resources to recognition, accountability, and later interpretation. Across them, ask whether influence serves the work, is visible enough to examine, and remains open to correction.

## Use the framework to guide inquiry

The 12 chapters examine 60 recurring patterns. Their names help identify what to investigate; they do not prove a case.

Scheduling errors, overload, confidentiality, urgency, and changing conditions may explain a missed invitation, late paper, difficult assignment, or revised measure. Establish what fits the event.

Give priority to consequential, repeated, unevenly distributed, or poorly explained events, especially departures from an announced rule. These warrant inquiry before a verdict.

Combine the introduction’s six inquiry questions into three passes. **Describe** the process, applicable rule, and practical consequences. **Test** comparable cases and alternative explanations. **Respond** with a correction and check whether it works. The passes organise the same method for use in practice.

### Pass one: describe

Begin with what an ordinary record could support. Identify the event, date, participants or roles, process, decision, and material effect. Separate what you observed from what someone else reported. Quote only words you are entitled to record, and only when exact wording matters.

“The paper arrived at 9:42 for a 10:00 decision, while two members had reviewed a draft the previous day” is a description. “They ambushed us” is an interpretation.

State the applicable rule, any departure, and the practical effect. If a figure changed, show both versions. This gives another person something specific to answer.

### Pass two: test

Ask what legitimate purpose, constraint, mistake, or competing interpretation could explain the event. Identify the evidence that would distinguish among those explanations.

Was access limited to protect privacy, or because challenge was inconvenient? Was the deadline short because of an external obligation, or because a preferred group had already prepared? Did a manager reassign work to meet demand, or to burden someone who spoke up? Did the goal change because conditions changed, or because the original goal was missed?

Use the tests to choose a remedy. An overloaded approval chain and deliberate withholding require different responses, even if both produced a late paper. Explaining the cause does not excuse the harm.

### Pass three: respond

Choose the smallest safe intervention capable of improving the decision or protecting people. It might be a clarification, corrected record, named owner, extended deadline, independent check, changed control, or formal protected report.

A small intervention can be powerful. The question “Who is authorised to decide this, and where will the reason be recorded?” can change how a meeting proceeds. Proportionality must not become passivity. Serious safety risk, violence, harassment, discrimination, retaliation, fraud, safeguarding concerns, privacy breaches, or evidence destruction may require immediate specialist or external action under applicable rules.

Use the three passes flexibly. Sometimes immediate harm must be addressed before the description is complete. Sometimes an intervention produces new evidence. Return to description, testing, and response as the facts change.

## Six records that make influence easier to examine

Six modest records connect influence to decisions people can examine. Use them within existing work rather than treating a values programme as the only response to mistrust.

### 1. The decision brief

Before a consequential choice, state:

- the decision required and its deadline
- the authorised decision-maker
- the criteria and constraints
- the people or roles whose evidence is needed
- the credible options
- the major assumptions and uncertainties
- the consultation already completed
- the record and communication that will follow

The brief need not be long. Writing it before the choice makes an unannounced change of forum, criteria, or options easier to see.

### 2. The information register

Identify the decision’s material sources, including their version, owner, access group, and limitations. Record when important information was provided and when a material correction was made.

Limit the register to material sources and corrections. Follow retention, confidentiality, security, and legal requirements. It should identify the evidence used, without becoming a surveillance archive or collection of private messages.

### 3. The participation map

List who decides, who provides specialist evidence, who performs the work, who bears material consequences, who has a formal right to consultation, and who needs notice. For each group, state how they will participate and what response or explanation they will receive afterwards.

This map tests meaningful involvement. A job title on a calendar entry does not establish that the person was consulted.

### 4. The contribution record

At major milestones, identify the people and teams responsible for outputs and critical enabling work. Include design, execution, coordination, risk prevention, repair, maintenance, and knowledge transfer. Let contributors correct material omissions.

Use the record for learning, recognition, evaluation, and staffing. Keep it proportionate: accurate attribution does not require a minute-by-minute ledger of personal ownership.

### 5. The action and accountability map

For each action, name the required result, owner, authority, resources, dependencies, due date, evidence of completion, verifier if needed, and route for resolving blockers. Assigning an action to someone who lacks the means to complete it creates a basis for future blame rather than accountability.

### 6. The outcome account

After the work, compare the result with the original promise and any approved changes. Show benefits, harms, trade-offs, missing data, material dissent, and the next decision. Distinguish delivery from benefit and fact from interpretation.

These records could be separate one-page documents, sections of a single brief, or fields in an existing system. Their value depends on use. A template nobody reads adds paperwork without improving transparency.

## What individuals can do

Individuals can clarify work within their roles even when they cannot redesign governance.

Start with process questions: “What decision are we making today?” “Which version is final?” “Who owns the exception?” “What evidence will close this?” “Is this consultation or approval?” These questions are less accusatory than claims about motive, yet often reveal the same structural problem.

Confirm important agreements and invite correction: “My understanding is that Priya will decide by Friday; my team will provide cost estimates; security approval is required above the stated threshold.” Make the record useful for current work.

Distinguish disagreement from noncompliance. You can state a concern, identify the deciding authority, ask that a material risk be recorded, and then implement a lawful and safe decision. If a protected duty or serious harm may be involved, use the relevant route. Ordinary meeting closure may not be sufficient.

Build broad, honest relationships. Ask colleagues how work reaches them, what constraints they face, and what evidence they trust. Sponsorship and coalitions are legitimate ways to build support. That support is more defensible when it concerns a stated idea and remains open to relevant challenge.

Keep records in proportion to the need. Maintain your work products, decisions, feedback, and approved commitments in authorised systems. Do not secretly collect colleagues’ personal information, forward confidential material to yourself, record conversations unlawfully, or search outside your access for proof. When the stakes rise, seek qualified advice about preservation and reporting.

Protect your energy. Not every awkward meeting signals a pattern worth pursuing. Choose issues by their consequence, recurrence, and your capacity to affect them. A framework intended to help you act should not turn every interaction into a private investigation.

## What managers can do on an ordinary Tuesday

Managers have disproportionate influence because they turn broad policy into invitations, deadlines, assignments, feedback, and explanations. These small choices build fair systems.

Before a meeting, send the question and materials early enough for the actual audience to prepare. Ask whose knowledge is missing. Disclose any material change made during earlier discussion.

During the meeting, clarify whether the group is exploring, advising, recommending, approving, or deciding. Invite evidence before announcing consensus. Stop disrespect without requiring every challenger to appear composed and speak fluently. If time forces closure, state what has not been heard and how it can still be submitted.

Afterwards, record the decision, owner, reasons, conditions, and trigger for reopening it. Assign actions only after confirming control and resources. Communicate with affected people without overstating agreement.

In one-to-one conversations, turn reputational labels into specific behaviour and expectations. Ask who contributed to visible outcomes. Allocate development opportunities through stated criteria. When someone raises a concern, explain what you can do, what you cannot promise, and what happens next. Handle changes to workload or opportunity carefully when they could reasonably appear to be consequences for speaking up.

In planning, identify critical knowledge and backups. Give experts time to teach. Examine whether your own convenience has made one person indispensable or one favoured deputy the inevitable successor.

Fair process supports relevant evidence, correction, and reasoned decisions even when the outcome is unpopular. Psychological safety enables candour but does not remove accountability or guarantee comfort.[^4]

## What leaders and governing bodies must own

Leaders must address incentives, allocation rules, and reporting independence. Individuals cannot make a complaint route independent by wording their concerns more carefully, or secure credit for maintenance when rewards recognise only launches.

Senior leaders and governing bodies must take responsibility for how the system works:

**Mandates.** Give forums and roles clear authority, boundaries, membership, and review. Retire bodies that absorb discussion without deciding.

**Criteria.** Publish how significant opportunities, resources, promotions, exceptions, and priorities are determined. Preserve judgement, but require reasons connected to purpose.

**Data governance.** Establish reliable sources, access based on need, correction routes, security, retention, and privacy. Transparency does not mean unrestricted visibility.

**Voice and protection.** Provide accessible ordinary, confidential, urgent, and protected routes. Define how matters will be handled, how conflicts and independence will be addressed, how closure will be provided, and how safeguards against retaliation will operate under applicable law and policy.

**Accountability.** Align expected outcomes with authority and resources. Separate learning, discipline, assurance, and legal processes when their purposes differ. Require senior decision-makers to explain their decisions to the same standard expected of others.

**Continuity.** Identify concentrated rights and knowledge, test absence, prepare for succession, and reward transfer. Repair systems that depend on exceptional individual effort.

**Learning.** Review near misses, successful recoveries, appeals, abandoned processes, and unintended effects. Change the design; revising the policy’s wording alone is insufficient.

Oversight should require evidence that these arrangements work. How long do appeals take? Who abandons the reporting route? How often do late papers alter decisions? Which critical activities have tested backups? How often are success measures changed? Counts alone cannot establish fairness, but they can show where further inquiry is needed.

## Fairness has four dimensions

People often disagree about fairness because they are discussing different things.

**Outcome fairness** asks who receives benefit, burden, recognition, or consequence.

**Process fairness** asks whether decisions are reached consistently, accurately, with opportunities for voice and correction, with controls against bias, and through ethical conduct.

**Interpersonal fairness** asks whether people are treated with dignity and respect.

**Explanatory fairness** asks whether reasons and information are truthful, timely, and sufficient.

Organisational-justice research connects these dimensions with attitudes and behaviour.[^1] Use them to choose the repair: revisit an allocation, correct the procedure, address disrespect, or provide missing reasons. A favourable result can still leave the other three dimensions unresolved.

Examine the rule and result together. Consistency can preserve an unjust rule; politeness can mask exclusion; a correct result can conceal a process that succeeded by luck.

Relevant differences may justify different treatment: safety credentials, incident severity, confidential evidence, or scarce funds. Explain the purpose and review route.

## Use influence responsibly

Use influence to defend worthwhile work and support people with less power. Pursuing advantage alone can damage the cooperation that makes influence useful.

Research on political skill examines social awareness, relationships, apparent sincerity, and adaptation to context.[^2] These capacities may connect knowledge, resolve conflict, and build support—or conceal self-serving conduct. Judge their use by purpose, means, truthfulness, and accountability.

Design fair consideration into the process so it remains available to people with different levels of influence.

## Do not turn the framework into a weapon

Apply the framework to your own use of influence as well as others’. Four restraints keep it useful.

First, describe the conduct before naming a pattern. Give the other person something specific to answer.

Second, include your own role. What information did you hold? Which deadline did you impose? What opportunity for clarification did you offer? Which preferred outcome may be shaping your interpretation?

Third, allow correction. When new evidence changes the explanation, update both your view and the record. Integrity does not require loyalty to an earlier accusation.

Fourth, choose an audience suited to the purpose. A process concern may belong with the decision owner rather than in a group chat. Take serious allegations to authorised channels instead of seeking agreement through a social campaign.

Never use the framework to diagnose personalities, infer secret intent, rank colleagues’ morality, or assemble unofficial dossiers. Its purpose is to improve work and identify when qualified review is needed.

## When informal repair is not enough

Move beyond informal repair when the risk or failure warrants it.

Consider formal or specialist support when harm is serious or repeated; when there is a strong power imbalance or threatened consequence; or when the issue involves protected reporting, discrimination or harassment, violence, safety or safeguarding, privacy or security, fraud, professional misconduct, evidence destruction, or a legal or contractual deadline.

Depending on the organisation and jurisdiction, support may come from a manager outside the issue, human resources, an employee representative or union, an ombuds or ethics function, safeguarding or safety leads, privacy or security officers, professional bodies, regulators, law enforcement, or qualified legal or clinical advisers.

Understand a route’s purpose before using it. A confidential adviser may help you think without opening a case; a mandatory reporter may have to act. An anonymous route may limit feedback. A grievance process, whistleblowing route, safety report, and emergency service are not interchangeable.

Preserve records lawfully and minimally. Put immediate safety first. Do not promise secrecy on behalf of a process you do not control. Do not confront someone if confrontation could increase danger or compromise evidence. This book offers questions for examining process, not legal advice or an investigation protocol.

## A twelve-week reset

Start by choosing one consequential, recurring decision and improving its entire process. An organisation need not implement 12 chapters at once.

### Weeks 1–2: choose and map

Select a decision important enough to matter but bounded enough to study, such as project prioritisation, promotion nominations, high-risk change approval, resource allocation, or complaint triage. Map its path from the first information to the final account. Mark where access, information, agenda, forum, resources, credit, accountability, challenge, continuity, and reporting shape the outcome.

Collect only authorised and necessary evidence. Speak with people who decide, contribute, perform the work, and bear the consequences. Ask where the work actually happens as well as what the policy says.

### Weeks 3–4: define the decision

Confirm the authority, criteria, options, required evidence, affected roles, timetable, conflicts, and review route. Write the short decision brief. Remove requirements that serve no real purpose. Add safeguards for privacy, security, accessibility, and representation.

### Weeks 5–8: run the process

Circulate information in time for people to use it. Track material corrections. Seek missing knowledge. Distinguish recommendation from approval. Record material changes and final decisions. Watch for burdens or dependencies that prevent people from using the opportunity to participate.

Assign actions to people with the necessary authority and resources. Record contributions while they are still visible. When urgency requires departure from the agreed process, state the reason and set a review.

### Weeks 9–10: account for the outcome

Compare the result with the original promise. Include trade-offs and unintended effects. Ask people who used or received the process what they found hard to access or understand. Protect confidentiality, and do not present a small voluntary sample as representative of everyone.

### Weeks 11–12: repair and standardise

Correct records, ownership, access, and controls. Keep only the artefacts that proved useful. Name who will maintain the process and when it will be reviewed. Tell participants what changed, what did not, and why. Identify one remaining concentration or inequity for the next cycle.

The goal is a tested local process that people can understand, use, and improve.

## How to tell whether trust is improving

Combine perceptions with behaviour and process evidence. One engagement question cannot separate trust in a manager from trust in a reporting route, or temporary satisfaction from dependable experience.

**Predictability.** Before a decision, can participants identify who decides, which criteria apply, and when they will receive an answer? Showing them the answer afterwards does not test predictability.

**Access.** Are relevant roles and affected groups reaching the process early enough to matter? Examine invitations, submissions, accommodations, and abandonment without constructing intrusive profiles.

**Information quality.** Are materials timely, current, sourced, and corrected when needed? Track material late changes and version disputes. Page counts do not establish quality.

**Challenge response.** What happens to inconvenient questions? Examine acknowledgement, routing, closure, recurrence, and subsequent treatment. Reports may increase when trust improves because people become more willing to speak; a lower count is not automatically a success.

**Decision integrity.** Do records accurately name authority, reasons, conditions, and dissent? Sample decisions and compare the final communication with the underlying record.

**Correction.** Can people correct material errors? Measure access to appeals, time to correction, repeated failures, and whether corrected records reach the places affected by the original mistake.

**Distribution.** Who repeatedly receives opportunity, sponsorship, credit, resources, burden, and blame? Make lawful and relevant comparisons, then examine what produced the differences. A difference prompts inquiry; it does not prove unfairness.

**Continuity.** Can critical work survive an absence? Examine tested backups, successful exercises, current guides, and concentrated access. A succession list alone is insufficient.

Use these records alongside carefully designed interviews, focus groups, surveys, and observation. State the purpose, protect privacy, limit retention, and never use trust research to identify or punish critics. If employees believe a survey can be traced to them, a high score may reflect caution rather than confidence.

Interpret changes in context. More corrected decisions might mean the underlying process deteriorated or the correction route became usable. More recorded dissent might signal greater conflict or more honest minutes. Faster closure might conceal superficial handling. Read measures together rather than expecting perfection.

Share findings with the people whose experience produced them. Explain what changed, which constraints remain, and when the next review will occur. Findings that stay in a senior report give participants no account of what the review learned.

Look for repeated agreement between promises and practice: the named owner decides, difficult challenges receive answers, and people who give early warnings remain able to contribute. Trust grows through that experience.

## The final test: can the system explain itself?

A trustworthy influence system can answer 12 ordinary questions:

- How did this issue receive attention?
- Who had access to the relevant information?
- Who shaped the options and criteria?
- Who was heard, and in what capacity?
- Who was authorised to decide?
- Which resources and dependencies mattered?
- Who contributed, and who received recognition?
- Who must answer for the result, and what did they control?
- Which reputational claims affected the choice?
- How could someone challenge or correct it safely?
- Could the work continue if a key person left?
- How was success defined, and did that definition change?

Disclosure has necessary limits: privacy, confidentiality, privilege, security, safety, and legitimate commercial or public interests. The answers should exist at the appropriate level, be known to authorised owners, and be available for proper review. They need not be disclosed to everyone.

Trust grows when people can predict the process, understand relevant reasons, correct material errors, and distinguish an unpopular decision from an arbitrary one. It does not require faith that every leader is benevolent or every colleague objective.

## Noor’s next meeting

At the next service committee meeting, the executives still arrive with a preferred option. This time, the paper discloses that they met in advance and explains the assumption they changed. A frontline scheduling lead points out that one option would shift delay from the central queue to local teams. The chair gives the programme time to check the data.

The committee does not reach consensus. Eight members prefer the programme’s recommendation, two prefer a phased version, and two recommend waiting until the workload effect can be measured. Under the charter, Noor is the decision-maker. She chooses the phased option, records the minority concerns, assigns the programme director to delivery and the operations director to the workload measure, and sets a threshold for reconsideration.

The result is unpopular with some members, but evidence, authority, consequences, and learning remain visible.

Politics remains, and people still have interests. They can now examine influence, challenge its effects, and repair the process. Influence is acknowledged and audited to help make collective judgement possible and worthy of trust.
