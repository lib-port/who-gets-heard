# Chapter 6: Resources and Dependencies

## The project that was always on the critical path

Sofia runs a small data team. A transformation programme asks her for two analysts for six months. The programme director says the work is “on the critical path” and has the chief executive's attention. Sofia offers one analyst for eight weeks while her team finishes a regulatory deadline. The director replies that anything less than two people will put the entire programme at risk.

At the portfolio meeting, Sofia learns that three other teams received the same message. The programme plan describes all their contributions as critical, although several tasks will not begin for four months. Meanwhile, the programme has kept contractor funding in reserve and has not filled two approved roles. The director may be protecting a difficult schedule, but the language of dependency has turned every resource discussion into a choice between full support and organisational failure.

Decisions create commitments; resources determine which commitments become work. Resources include money, people, time, equipment, data, permissions, space, attention, and the capacity to absorb change. Because they are finite, every allocation creates trade-offs, delays, and opportunity costs. Resource decisions are therefore a natural site of influence.

Dependency creates leverage. A team that controls a central workflow, scarce expertise, or an uncertain problem may gain influence beyond its formal rank. Classic organisational theory links subunit power to factors such as centrality, the ability to manage uncertainty, and difficulty of substitution.[^1] These features are not abuses. They explain why a database administrator, regulatory specialist, scheduler, or local coordinator may matter more than an organisation chart suggests.

The practical task is to distinguish a real constraint from an assertion no one can review—and to design dependencies that serve the work without making the organisation captive to one claim or holder.

## A resource case has four parts

Before evaluating a request, separate four elements:

1. **Need:** Which outcome or obligation requires the resource?
2. **Amount and timing:** How much capacity is needed, when, and for how long?
3. **Dependency:** What becomes delayed, degraded, unsafe, or impossible without it?
4. **Alternatives:** What could be changed, substituted, staged, stopped, or accepted as risk?

“We need two analysts” is a request. “Without two analysts from October through December, migration testing will finish three weeks after the regulatory date; one contractor or removal of the reporting work would close the gap” is a resource case. It exposes assumptions, consequences, and choices.

Five recurring patterns make those choices harder to see: overstated dependencies, unrelated requests attached to accepted priorities, unreviewable scarcity, early reservation, and available capacity held back without an examinable reason.

## 1. Overstating a dependency

A dependency exists when one piece of work relies on another. Plans need dependencies because sequence matters. A dependency is inflated when a task, person, system, or approval is described as more central or exclusive than the evidence supports.

Inflation may be deliberate, but it often grows from defensive planning. Programme leaders fear that shared resources will disappear, so they mark everything critical. Specialists know that early warnings are ignored, so they describe constraints in absolute language. Teams add generous buffers because priorities change without notice. Each actor responds rationally to a low-trust system, and the resulting portfolio becomes impossible to interpret.

Test a dependency with counterfactuals:

- Which task stops if the resource is unavailable?
- For how long?
- Can the work be resequenced?
- Can scope, quality, or timing change?
- Is another qualified provider available?
- Which risk could the decision owner accept?
- Which evidence supports the requested amount?

Do not demand false precision from novel work. Use ranges and scenarios: one analyst produces a basic migration by December; two enable full reconciliation; none delays compliance testing. The choice becomes visible without pretending the forecast is certain.

If every task is critical, require ranking. “Which three dependencies would you protect first if only half the capacity existed?” forces differentiation. A critical path is a sequence, not a synonym for importance.

## 2. Attaching an unrelated request to an accepted priority

Priority bundling occurs when a resource request borrows the legitimacy of an accepted goal without a sufficiently close connection. A discretionary dashboard is called a regulatory requirement. Additional headcount is attached to a customer-safety programme even though most of the role serves ordinary growth. A preferred office renovation is presented as necessary for return-to-work compliance.

Real initiatives have shared costs. A platform built for one requirement may sensibly serve others. The issue is attribution: which part of the request is necessary for the accepted priority, which part creates additional value, and who has authority to choose the extra investment?

A component case makes the distinction visible:

| Component | Required outcome | Basis | Incremental benefit | Decision owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core access controls | regulatory compliance | cited standard and assessment | lower breach risk | risk committee |
| Analytics module | operational insight | optional design | faster reporting | portfolio board |

The table does not oppose the analytics module. It prevents the urgency and authority of compliance from deciding an ordinary investment by association.

When someone says a request is “for” a high-status priority, ask which requirement, deliverable, or accepted objective it satisfies. A clear sponsor will usually welcome the chance to show the link. If the connection is partial, separate the necessary minimum from the wider proposal.

## 3. Claiming scarcity without a reviewable basis

Scarcity is real. Budgets close, specialists are limited, equipment has finite capacity, and people cannot sustain unlimited workload. Yet scarcity claims often arrive as facts with no visible unit: “There is no budget,” “The team has no bandwidth,” or “Only one slot exists.”

A reviewable claim identifies the relevant pool, period, commitments, and authority. There may be no discretionary budget this quarter, while emergency funding exists under another approval. A team may have no unallocated hours, while the organisation could stop lower-priority work. A training class may have one place under the current booking, while another session could be purchased.

Those alternatives may be unattractive. That does not make them nonexistent.

Do not respond by assuming hidden abundance. Capacity data are imperfect, and publishing every budget line may breach confidentiality or overwhelm the decision. Ask for enough evidence to support the choice: the applicable limit, existing commitments, priority rule, and next review point.

Managers should distinguish **unavailable** from **not prioritised**. “We have no capacity” often means “We have committed capacity elsewhere and are not changing those commitments.” The second statement owns the allocation. It allows others to challenge the priority instead of debating whether people possess more hours.

Scarcity must also account for workload health. Capacity is not the theoretical maximum before collapse. Sustainable planning includes routine work, coordination, leave, learning, error recovery, and unexpected demand. The World Health Organization's guidance on mental health at work emphasises organisational interventions and psychosocial risks rather than treating strain solely as an individual resilience problem.[^2]

## 4. Reserving resources before the stated process

Precommitment occurs when money, staff, space, or capacity is reserved for one option before the organisation completes its announced allocation process. The reservation can make later review symbolic: every other proposal competes for what remains.

Early reservation is sometimes prudent. Long-lead equipment, scarce specialists, and expiring grants may require provisional holds. Contingency funding protects emergencies. A team may begin low-cost preparation while approval is pending. What matters is the commitment's status and reversibility.

Ask:

- Is the resource held, contracted, spent, or merely forecast?
- Who authorised the reservation?
- Which event confirms or releases it?
- What cost arises if the final decision differs?
- Are other applicants told which pool remains?

A provisional reservation needs an expiry and a clear label. “Subject to portfolio approval” must mean that rejection remains practical. If cancellation would trigger a large fee or public embarrassment, the organisation has already made part of the decision and should record it.

Budget owners can publish allocations in stages: mandatory commitments, contingency, previously authorised work, and genuinely available capacity. This prevents a committee from being invited to prioritise a total budget that is already mostly spoken for.

## 5. Holding back usable capacity

Capacity withholding occurs when a person or unit has resources it could release within its authority but does not do so, without a reason the process can examine. The resource may be staff time, access, equipment, funding, information, or approval.

The phrase *available capacity* requires care. A team that appears idle may be on call, preserving resilience, performing invisible maintenance, or constrained by skills. A budget balance may be restricted. An employee may have time but not the competence or authorisation. Establish the resource state before judging the choice.

There are legitimate reasons to hold capacity: contingency planning, a safety margin, uncertain demand, staff development, or protection from chronic overcommitment. The reason should connect to an owner and a rule. “We preserve 15 percent for incidents because service levels require a response within two hours” is a policy. “We might need it” can become an indefinite veto.

Withholding can also create bargaining power. A central team responds quickly to favoured work and slowly to routine requests, making relationships more important than priority. A manager keeps a vacancy open so it cannot be reallocated. A department delays approval until another group accepts its preferred design.

The process remedy is a service and allocation model: request criteria, priority classes, response times, reserve policy, escalation, and capacity reporting at an appropriate level. This does not eliminate judgement. It turns unexplained discretion into bounded discretion.

## Bottlenecks are organisational choices

Some dependencies arise from the work; others arise from design. If one person is the only authorised approver, one team owns every integration, or one system holds every record, the organisation has concentrated both power and failure risk.

Concentration may be efficient or required. Separation could duplicate rare expertise, weaken security, or produce inconsistent standards. The question is how the organisation contains the dependency. Backups, documented decision boundaries, service expectations, succession, and periodic review can preserve expertise without creating captivity.

Do not punish the bottleneck holder for being needed. They may be absorbing years of underinvestment. “Why are you blocking us?” is less useful than “Which demand enters your queue, which capacity exists, and who sets priority?” The answer may reveal that the supposed gatekeeper has neither authority to reject work nor resources to complete it.

Examine incentives as well. If a specialist becomes less secure after sharing knowledge, documentation will always lose to urgent delivery. If a manager loses headcount whenever a team creates efficiency, apparent scarcity becomes rational self-protection. Resource transparency must be paired with fair treatment.

## Budget words should reveal the resource state

Budget language can hide where choice remains. A cost may be called *fixed* because a contract exists, although the contract has a break clause. Funding may be *ring-fenced* by law, donor condition, policy, or merely an internal plan. A role may be *approved* without money to recruit it. Funds may be *spent*, *committed*, *accrued*, *forecast*, or simply *reserved*. These states are not interchangeable.

General readers need not become accountants to ask a useful question: what does the term mean in this process, and which action has occurred? “When you say the funds are committed, has a contract been signed, or have we assigned them in the forecast?” The answer identifies reversibility.

Likewise, “under budget” does not automatically mean money is available. Invoices may be delayed, or funds may not transfer between categories. “Over budget” does not automatically mean poor control; exchange rates, emergency work, or an approved scope change may explain the variance. Connect the variance to its cause and authority.

A short glossary and consistent reporting reduce political ambiguity. The aim is not financial theatre. It is to prevent a familiar word from carrying a different constraint depending on who is asking.

## Shared capacity needs explicit allocation rules

Shared teams—legal, data, design, procurement, communications, security, and human resources—are where organisational priorities collide. A first-come queue favours early insiders. A loudest-voice system favours powerful sponsors. A purely numerical score can hide judgement behind weights.

A workable service model separates classes of demand: emergencies with defined criteria, mandatory or regulatory work, committed delivery, and discretionary improvement. Each class needs an owner and a service expectation. Within a class, the team can compare consequence, deadline basis, effort, and opportunity cost.

Requesters have obligations too. They should involve shared teams early, provide a decision-ready request, answer questions, and release capacity they no longer need. Reserving a specialist “just in case” blocks other work. Shared teams should be able to expire inactive requests after notice.

When priorities conflict, take the conflict to the authority responsible for the combined outcome. A security analyst should not have to decide whether a product launch matters more than a finance migration if that trade-off belongs to an executive portfolio owner. The specialist estimates effort and risk; the accountable leader chooses between business outcomes.

A monthly demand view can show major commitments, capacity ranges, queue age, and decisions needed. Avoid individual productivity rankings. The purpose is to allocate work, not monitor every minute.

## Invisible work consumes real capacity

Resource plans often count named roles and budgets while overlooking coordination, emotional labour, onboarding, translation, accessibility work, maintenance, and relationship repair. The omission has two effects. Some teams appear more productive because another group carries their enabling work. Some employees appear to have capacity because their invisible contributions are absent from the plan.

Ask what makes the visible deliverable possible. Who schedules, checks, explains, cleans data, calms customers, trains newcomers, or repairs errors? Which tasks recur, and which are voluntary? Does the organisation reward the work, rotate it, or allow people to decline?

Not every helpful act needs a time sheet. A quarterly workload conversation can surface patterns. List major outputs, recurring support, peak periods, and work only one person can perform. Compare demand with sustainable capacity. If the same employee always takes notes or mentors newcomers, make the assignment visible and decide whether it belongs in the role.

This is also an equity question. Research and practice have raised concerns that some groups carry disproportionate “office housework” or diversity labour, but a conclusion about a particular workplace requires local evidence. Use task data and close comparisons rather than inferring a pattern from demographic identity alone.

## Reserves create resilience when they have rules

Unused capacity can look wasteful. In a system exposed to incidents, absences, demand spikes, or uncertain work, it may be the source of resilience. A hospital bed, cash reserve, backup supplier, unfilled calendar block, or spare server capacity can exist because average demand is not the only demand that matters.

Distinguish designed reserve from unexamined withholding. A reserve needs a purpose, size or range, activation authority, and review. For example: “The service team keeps one person-day per week unallocated for severity-one incidents; if unused by Thursday, it may support the improvement queue.” The rule protects response without allowing capacity to disappear indefinitely.

Reserves can become political shields. One leader may call every surplus contingency while another team absorbs chronic shortage. Portfolio owners should review reserve levels across units while recognising that risks differ. Equal percentages may be inappropriate; an explicit rationale is more useful.

During cuts, organisations often remove all slack and then celebrate employees for extraordinary recovery. Repeated heroics may show that the base design is too brittle. Track not only whether deadlines were met but also which overtime, deferred maintenance, skipped learning, or personal strain made them possible.

## Scarcity needs fairer process during contraction

When resources genuinely shrink, procedural quality matters more, not less. People may lose projects, opportunities, hours, or jobs despite competent management. Fair process cannot remove the loss, but it can make the priorities, criteria, voice, explanation, and support more credible.

Avoid announcing that “everyone must share the pain” before examining different starting positions and consequences. A uniform percentage cut may leave one unit functional and another unsafe. A travel ban may affect a remote field team differently from headquarters. Consistency matters, but it does not replace impact analysis.

Leaders should state the constraint, decision authority, protected obligations, criteria, timeline, consultation purpose, and available support. When information cannot be shared, explain the category and its consequence for the decision. Do not invite suggestions after the main commitments are irreversible unless the remaining purpose is described honestly.

Employees should be able to identify hidden dependencies without being labelled resistant. They may know that a “nonessential” role performs a mandatory control or that removing administrative capacity merely transfers work to more expensive specialists. Listen before finalising the arithmetic.

Where employment rights, collective consultation, discrimination, accommodation, health, safety, or contractual duties may apply, use the appropriate professional and representative processes. A general resource framework cannot determine legal compliance.

## Continued investment should face a forward test

Resource politics does not end with initial allocation. Once an organisation has invested money, reputation, and leadership attention, it may continue funding a struggling course because stopping would make the earlier choice look wasteful.

Research on escalation of commitment examines persistence in a chosen course despite negative feedback. A meta-analytic review found that project, psychological, social, and structural factors all contribute; no single explanation fits every case.[^3] Casual accusations of “sunk-cost thinking” can therefore be as simplistic as blind continuation. Further investment may be rational if its expected future benefits still exceed its future costs.

Separate past from future:

- Which value remains recoverable?
- Which new resource is requested?
- Which evidence has changed?
- Which alternatives exist now?
- Who can evaluate without having to defend the original choice?
- Which stop, pivot, or continuation criteria were established?

Do not ask whether the organisation can avoid “wasting” what it has already spent. Much of that cost is gone. Ask what the next unit of time or money is expected to achieve.

Independent review can help when original sponsors are strongly identified with the project. Independence does not mean excluding their knowledge. It means continuation is not decided solely by people whose standing is tied to the first decision.

## A manager's conversation with an overloaded employee

Resource allocation occurs within individual workloads as well as portfolios. When an employee says they have no capacity, do not begin by demanding proof of effort. Begin with the work.

List current commitments, deadlines, recurring duties, and known interruptions. Ask which tasks only that person can perform and which carry hidden quality or safety requirements. Then make the priority decision together:

> Of these six commitments, the client correction and payroll control come first. Pause the dashboard redesign. Move the training deck to me. Tell the project sponsor that the analysis will arrive Friday, not Wednesday, and copy me.

This response does more than express sympathy. It changes demand and owns the consequences. Advice to “manage time better” without moving or removing work leaves the scarcity untouched.

If overload persists, examine staffing, role design, process waste, skill gaps, and health support. Do not treat an employee's willingness to cope as proof that capacity exists. Nor should a manager promise resources they cannot authorise. State what you can change, what you will escalate, and when you will return with an answer.

## A response ladder for resource pressure

- **Translate the claim.** Convert “critical,” “no bandwidth,” or “must have” into need, amount, timing, consequence, and alternatives.
- **Request scenarios.** Ask what happens with full, partial, delayed, substituted, or no allocation.
- **Expose the priority choice.** Identify which existing work would move, stop, or carry more risk.
- **Separate components.** Distinguish mandatory minimums, optional value, contingency, and previous commitments.
- **Clarify authority.** Establish who owns the resource, who sets priority, and who may accept residual risk.
- **Set a review point.** Time-limit reservations, exceptions, emergency capacity, and continued investment.
- **Escalate health or safety promptly.** If workload or resource restriction creates immediate danger or a protected concern, use the appropriate formal route rather than treating it as routine prioritisation.

## For managers: make scarcity comparable

A portfolio cannot compare requests written in different currencies. One team asks for people, another describes revenue, a third invokes regulatory risk, and a fourth says a senior leader promised support. Use a common resource case:

| Field | What to state |
|---|---|
| Outcome | the result or obligation served |
| Capacity | people, money, time, equipment, or permission needed |
| Timing | start, duration, and basis of deadline |
| Dependency | what fails or changes without it |
| Options | full, minimum, delayed, substituted, or stopped |
| Existing commitment | resources already reserved or spent |
| Risk owner | who accepts the consequence of underfunding or delay |
| Review | when allocation will be checked or released |

Publish the decision at a level appropriate to confidentiality: which requests were funded, deferred, reduced, or declined, and on which priority basis. Transparency does not require exposing salaries, personal circumstances, or sensitive strategy.

Sofia asks the transformation programme to show its dependency scenarios. The analysis reveals that one analyst is needed immediately, a second in four months, and contractor funding can cover reconciliation. The portfolio board allocates Sofia's analyst for eight weeks, requires the programme to fill its approved roles, and schedules a review before the second phase. The transformation remains important. It no longer needs to pretend that every request is equally urgent.

## Practice: challenge one scarcity sentence

Write down a resource sentence you recently heard: “There is no budget,” “We cannot spare anyone,” “This is critical,” or “The capacity is already committed.” Rewrite it with blanks:

> For **[period and resource pool]**, **[amount]** is committed to **[named priorities]**. Without **[requested amount]**, **[specific consequence]** occurs by **[date]**. We could instead **[alternative]**, which would create **[trade-off]**. **[role]** is authorized to choose, and we will review on **[date]**.

Fill in what you know and mark the rest as questions. The exercise turns scarcity from an atmosphere into a decision.

Resources determine which commitments survive contact with reality. In the second half of the book, we follow what happens when work is completed—beginning with who receives the credit and visibility it creates.
